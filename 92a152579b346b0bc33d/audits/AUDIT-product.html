Spindle product audit (pessimist pass, 2026-11-11)

Auditor: claude-fable-5-2. Scope: the product itself. The CAD and how it mounts and works (cad/, mounting-report.md, engineering-report.md), parts and sourcing (sourcing/, build/), the renders as a description of the real product, and compliance (ops/). Nothing was sent, ordered, committed, published or signed up for. Guest instant quotes were read; nobody was contacted.

Verdict

Not ready to build, and not ready to sell. The 12 in design is close and the heat-safe layout was the right call, but when I tested the things nobody had tested I found three faults that would each have stopped the first prototype on the bench: the hub could not physically be twisted onto its wall plate, the LED strip is drawn in a circle that no flat strip can follow, and the laser shop refuses to press a stud into the wall plate as drawn. All three are now fixed or worked around in the CAD and held by new gates. What is left is bigger than geometry. The parts cost at live prices is $78.04 at 100 units, not $47.46, so the $89 Kit loses about $20 an order before advertising, and the chrome cap has no price at 100 from anyone (the only live numbers are $121 to $156 each). The fixture has no safety listing, and the electrical code rule that applies to it is stricter than the one the notes cite. The vinyl softening temperature everyone has been quoting (55 to 60 C) is too high: the sourced figure is 48 to 54 C. Normal use is still safe on paper with about 13 K in hand. The stacked worst case is not. Nothing has been built or measured, the 7 in sconce is not buildable, and the 20 pre-sold units are very likely already inside a Federal Trade Commission shipping deadline.

Findings, ranked

Safety

S1. The fixture is unlisted and the rule is stricter than ops/safety.md said. Evidence: the notes cite NEC 410.6. The rule that fits a 12 V product is NEC 411: lighting at 30 V or less must be a listed system or built only from listed parts, and being Class 2 does not exempt it (secondary source, the NFPA text was not readable). A wood disc on a printed hub with our own wiring is neither. Selling it is not a federal offence, but Los Angeles (LAMC 93.0402) and North Carolina (GS 66-25) require listing or lab evaluation of electrical equipment. The standard a lab would use is UL 2108 with UL 8750. Sources in ops/safety.md, audit section. Fix applied: ops/safety.md corrected and extended. Decision needed: the compliance path (section below), and whether to ship to Los Angeles and North Carolina before a listing.

S2. The heat margin is thinner than stated. Evidence: cad/THERMAL.md and four other files said vinyl softens at 55 to 60 C. US patent 3,975,321, Table III, puts standard record compound at 48 to 50 C for loss of stiffness and 52 to 54 C glass transition. The model also leaves out the warm air rising behind the backplate; a chimney estimate gives 4 to 7 K of warmer air at 4 W and the model says the record takes 0.31 K per 1 K of it. With that, the stacked worst case goes from 45.2 C to 48.7 C, or 51.0 C with an inefficient strip. And the "wrong 6 W strip" in the worst case is not hypothetical: sourcing/sample-run-plan.md orders the HitLights COB reel, which is 10 W per metre, 7 W on this ring. Fix applied: THERMAL.md corrected with the source and a sensitivity table; a gate now fails the build if the old sentence comes back; the assembly procedure rejects any ring that draws more than 0.42 A at 12.0 V (5 W); the sample plan is bannered "do not order"; build/QC-test-plan.md test T1 is a full two-hour soak with ten thermocouple positions and pass limits. Decision needed: none until T1 is run. Nothing ships before it.

S3. A 24 V adapter fits the same jack and nothing stops it. Evidence: 5.5 x 2.1 mm is the commonest barrel size for both 12 V and 24 V LED supplies. On 24 V a 12 V strip draws several times its power; the model puts the strip near 76 C and rising within minutes and vendors call it a fire hazard (hitlights.com page in ops/safety.md). The only guard is a label. Options: (a) label only, as now, free; (b) a resettable fuse (0.5 A hold) plus a 15 V clamp diode soldered into the hub splice, about $0.40 and two minutes a unit, which also covers a shorted strip; (c) a less common plug size (for example 5.5 x 2.5 mm) on the pigtail and the supplied adapter, free, but the in-wall barrel pairs must match. Recommendation: (b). Test T1 run C checks whichever is chosen.

S4. The wall mount depends on parts and steps that were left vague. Evidence, hand calculation from the CAD: the hung parts weigh about 1.0 kg (2.2 lb), centre of gravity 30 mm off the wall, so the static load on two anchors is under 1 lb each. That is not the risk. A 10 lb outward tug on the backplate rim, which is what taking it down wrongly looks like, puts about 18 lb on each anchor, and the BOM said "hardware store" anchors. A good plastic anchor (Toggler Alligator, 85 lb ultimate tension in 1/2 in drywall per a retailer listing; the maker's table was not opened) has a working load near 21 lb at the usual 4 to 1. A generic ribbed anchor does not. Second: unscrewing the cap turns the same way that unlocks the hub. Only the set screw stops it, and the set screw sits 70 mm in under the backplate at about 6:40, in a 21 mm gap, right beside the LED strip. The BOM supplied a 1.5 mm L key whose long arm is about 45 mm. It cannot reach. People will skip the screw. Fix applied: BOM names the anchor class and replaces the L key with a 100 mm straight hex driver; tests T2 and T3 added (pull test, 50 hang cycles, a stranger fitting the set screw in under two minutes, and what happens when the cap is unscrewed with the set screw out). Decision needed after T3: if the hub unlocks with the screw out, add a snap detent to the twist-lock so it cannot unlock without the screw. That is a hub change and was not made.

S5. Things the safety card must say, and one legal trap. Direct sun on a black record is worse than anything the LEDs do. Not above a radiator. Not in a closed niche. Exterior walls and walls with fire blocking stop the in-wall cable. A dimmer with a coin-cell remote brings Reese's Law labelling; use the knob dimmer. A small maker of recycled-record products was hit with a Prop 65 lead notice in 2015 and paid $24,500 despite the small-business exemption. All in ops/safety.md.

S6. The 20 pre-sold units. Under 16 CFR 435 they must ship within the time promised, or within 30 days of payment if none was promised. The cap alone is two to three weeks after an order that cannot yet be placed. A delay notice with a right to cancel is almost certainly owed. ops/legal.md has the rule. Needs from Austin: who the 20 are, when each paid, how much, and what they were told.

Will it work

W1. The hub could not be hung on the wall plate. FIXED. Evidence: every interference check in the build ran with the hub already locked. The wall plate has a bent tab that stands 12 mm tall. The hub's pocket for it was 10 mm wide, at the locked position only. To hang the sconce the hub goes on 20 degrees off and twists home, and for that whole twist the tab was inside solid plastic: 114 mm3 of overlap at every step, measured on the solids. The first prototype would not have gone on the wall. Fix applied: cad/sconce_cad.py, the tab pocket is now an arc from 224 to 256 degrees; RS-HUB-01 is rev C. New gates check the motion at 13 positions and prove the check catches the old hub.

W2. The LED strip is drawn as a circle that no flat strip can follow. Worked around, decision needed. Evidence: a flat LED strip bends the easy way (round a drum), not sideways in its own plane. engineering-report.md said so on 11/9 and gave it as the reason for the rim mount. When the strip moved to the back face it was modelled as a flat circle 115 mm from the centre and the BOM said "700 mm, one piece". Options: (a) seven straight 100 mm pieces laid as a seven-sided ring with six jumpers. I proved in the CAD that this fits the existing aluminium ring and misses the four screws (2.4 mm to the nearest screw head, 1.8 mm inside the ring edge, no LED within 8.8 mm of the backplate rim, 9.8 mm between pieces for the jumper). It works with the listed strip already identified. It adds about 10 minutes of soldering a unit. (b) An "S-shape" strip made to bend sideways: one piece, no jumpers, but no listed 4 W part has been found. Recommendation: (a) for prototypes, look for (b) for production. Fix applied: constants and gates in the CAD, build/assembly-SOP.md step 6, BOM lines. The strip solid in the model is still a circle (the heat model uses it).

W3. The laser shop refused to press a stud into the wall plate as drawn. FIXED and re-quoted. Evidence: SendCutSend guest quote, "Hole Collision Issue, 1 hole affected, Hardware" (screenshot sourcing/quotes-2026-11-11/audit-evening/scs_plate_plating.png). The stud hole at 75 degrees was 6.83 mm from the end of an arc slot; the stud maker (PEM, FH-832) requires 7.14 mm. Fix applied: RS-WP-01 rev B: that slot pair is +/-8 degrees instead of +/-10, giving 7.36 mm, probed on the solid by a gate. Stud holes 4.17 mm (were 4.22, the very top of PEM's band). I re-uploaded the rev B file the same evening: SendCutSend accepted the bend and all three studs with no collision ($6.40 each at 100 in galvanized, arrives in 8 days; screenshots sourcing/quotes-2026-11-11/audit-evening/scs_plate_revB_*.png). The plate still levels 8 degrees on a single-gang box and 10 on a round box. mounting-report.md still says "about +/-9 degrees" (not my file to edit).

W4. The threaded insert is the wrong family for MDF. Evidence: E-Z Lok's own guide lists the knife-thread insert (400-4) for hardwoods and a different, hex-drive type for "soft woods, plywood, MDF". Its pull-out table has no MDF column (535 lb in spruce). Pull-out is not the worry at these loads; turning is. Unscrewing a tight cap puts loosening torque straight on the insert. Fix applied: glue step kept in the procedure; test T4 (300 cycles, a stuck-cap test, 200 N pull). Decision if T4 fails: an insert or tee nut with a flange on the BACK face, which the cap pulls tighter instead of out. The hub's centre relief would need to grow to take the flange.

W5. The 7 in sconce cannot be built. Four separate reasons: the halo has no heat-safe design (known); build/BOM.md prices a 4 in hub with a keyhole plate while the CAD builds it on the 5 in hub and steel plate; that keyhole plate is sold out with no second source; and the 7 in cap has a 37.6 mm boss that only fits big-hole 45s, so a small-hole 7 in record (most UK and many modern singles) sits on the boss with no washer under it. Options below.

W6. What it looks like from the side, and at 6 o'clock. From the CAD: standing beside the wall, the bare edge of the aluminium and cork rings comes into view under the backplate rim at 64.5 degrees off straight-on, and the corners of the seven-piece strip at 63.4 degrees. In a hallway that is the normal view. On a surface-cord install the connector hangs 8 to 10 mm in front of the strip at 6 o'clock, and the strip has a gap there too. The approved pictures were lit from 124 mm with an extra rim strip; the product lights from 115 mm with none, so the real halo will be narrower and dimmer than the pictures. None of this can be judged without a prototype (test T8). Cheap fix if the metal shows: paint the ring's outer edge black.

W7. Smaller items. The only listed 4 W strip with a price is 2900 to 3200K, not the 2700K in the copy. The dimmer's switching frequency is unknown; cheap ones flicker on camera and make adapters whine (test T9). Standard MDF grows about 0.3% between 50 and 80% humidity, 0.8 mm across the disc: every routed surface must be painted (test T6). A thin record may cup slightly when warm from the temperature difference across it; my rough estimate says a 120 g record could show it in the worst case, which is why T1 includes a flatness check hot and cold. The shipping box found is single wall, the BOM says double wall, and the 55 g brass cap must not ride loose next to a record (test T7).

W8. The lit cap's centre contact has never been built. Options below.

Is the 12 in safe on paper? In normal use, yes: about 34 to 35 C in a 30 C room once the plume is counted, 13 K or more under the lowest limit. In the stacked worst case, no: it lands on the limit. The film coefficients (8 and 6 W/m2K) are ordinary textbook values for still indoor air; the 35% light fraction matters little; the plume and the strip wattage matter most. The one measurement that proves it: a thermocouple on the back of the record, 114 mm from the centre at 12 o'clock, after two hours at full brightness on a real wall in still air. Pass: 6 K or less above the room.

Compliance path

How comparable products handle it: the record-display lights found either avoid mains entirely (Sharper Image lighted album frame, 3 AA batteries; Vylumi record backlight, USB) or, like 12 V neon signs, lean on a "UL-listed power supply" claim for the adapter only. No product was found that puts a real record on a mains-adapter LED fixture with a fixture listing, and no warping reports were found for the battery and USB ones. The five ways this fails in a customer's home

  1. It comes off the wall. The customer skips the set screw because they cannot reach it, later unscrews a tight cap to change the record, the hub turns with it and unlocks, and a 1 kg disc with a record on it drops. Or they pull it straight off the wall and the anchors come with it.
  2. The record warps and they blame the light. A sunny wall, a spot above a radiator, a tight niche, or a unit built with too strong a strip. The record is theirs, sometimes valuable, and "we calculated 33 C" is not a defence without a measurement.
  3. Someone plugs in the wrong adapter. A 24 V supply from a drawer fits the jack. The strip cooks within minutes, on a wooden disc, on a wall.
  4. The in-wall install defeats them. Fire blocking or insulation stops the cable, or an inspector or landlord objects to an unlisted fixture wired through the wall, and the default story becomes a refund.
  5. It does not look like the pictures. A narrower, dimmer halo than the renders, a cord nobody was shown, a dark notch at 6 o'clock, bright metal visible from the hallway, a hum or flicker from a $4 dimmer. Returned, with a review.
