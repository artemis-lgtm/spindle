1. Product and build

What exists today splits into three camps, and none of them is exactly the thing Austin described, which is the good news. The first camp is real-vinyl upcycled lighting on Etsy: stacks of 12" LPs formed into a pendant on a wooden wall arm with a white cable, inline switch and plug, listed at CA$146.21 (https://www.etsy.com/ca/listing/583317430/wall-light-upcycled-12lp-vinyl-record); a handmade "5 tier" upcycled record lamp at $80 and single-record hanging pendants at $22.50-$30 (https://www.etsy.com/market/vinyl_record_lamp); the CORONA pendant made from vinyl records at $115.13 (same market page); and a few one-off "battery operated personalized record wall sconce" makers who glue a battery puck behind a record (https://www.pinterest.com/pin/624241198664151026/, https://www.pinterest.de/pin/367536019567378180/). The second camp is mass-market resin fakes on Amazon and Walmart: the "Record Store Retro Wall Light" is a 5.9 x 2.17 x 7.09 inch resin miniature of a record in a frame, 3xAAA powered, selling around $20.89 at Walmart and about $22-32 on Amazon in several colourways (https://www.walmart.com/ip/Record-Store-Retro-Wall-Light-Vintage-Vinyl-Record-Wall-Decor-Music-Lover-Gift-Retro-Room-Aesthetic-Record-Collector-Gift/16764366840, https://www.amazon.com/Record-Store-Retro-Wall-Light/dp/B0FDQ2SYZW, https://www.amazon.com/Record-Wall-LED-Vinyl-Decor/dp/B0FMYBZS5T). The third camp is the "Now Playing" light-up display shelf (wood base, acrylic sheets, USB LED, remote) that holds your own record; it sells for $26.99 to $65.99 depending on the storefront and the YuanDian version carries a 4.4-star rating from thousands of reviews (https://www.billboard.com/culture/product-recommendations/light-up-vinyl-record-stand-now-playing-sign-buy-online-1235950991/, https://woodartsupply.com/products/ammonite-light-up-now-spinning-vinyl-record-stand-wall-mount-now-playing-record-player-stand-wooden-acrylic-holder-for-vinyl-album-display-and-storage-with-warm-white-color-lights, https://www.ebay.com/itm/366520633843). The gap is a finished, plug-in, real-record wall sconce that looks like a lighting fixture rather than a craft project, which is what Austin is proposing.

The build I recommend is a halo backlit sconce: the record stands off the wall by about 1 inch on a round plywood or MDF backplate slightly smaller than the record (so the backplate hides behind the vinyl), with a warm-white LED ring or strip on the backplate's edge throwing a halo onto the wall and the groove texture, plus an optional small puck or lens in the centre spindle hole. A braided cord drops to a plug with an inline switch. This gives a real "fixture" look, hides all electronics, and uses only low-voltage parts behind the record. Per unit, the bill of materials is: one 12" LP; one 11" backplate cut from 1/2" plywood or MDF (roughly $1.50-3 of sheet stock, a 2x4 ft 1/2" MDF handy panel is about $20 at any box store); a 12V low-voltage LED strip, about 3 ft of 2835 warm white (a UL-listed 16 ft reel runs $10-20, so about $2-4 per unit); a UL-listed 12V plug-in power supply with dimmer, the GXILEE UL Listed 12V 2A LED Power Supply with Dimmer Switch on Amazon is the archetype (https://www.amazon.com/Listed-Supply-Dimmer-Transformer-Connector/dp/B095HL6VL2; price did not render for the scraper, this class of adapter sells for roughly $12-16 single and $7-9 at 100 from the same brands' Alibaba storefronts, treat that as an estimate); keyhole hangers at $9.99 per 30 or $7.96 per 50 (https://www.amazon.com/keyhole-hanging-hardware/s?k=keyhole+hanging+hardware), so under $0.35 each; standoff spacers or felt pads, screws, two-part epoxy or VHB tape to fix the record to the backplate, about $1-2; and a 13x13x3 shipping box with foam corners, about $3-4 each at qty 25 from Uline or Amazon. If Austin prefers a simpler first unit, the two off-the-shelf alternatives are a plug-in 120V puck kit (the LEDMyPlace 3-puck kit is ETL listed, 120V, 74" cord with toggle switch, 3x140 lumens, $38.99 for three, so about $13 per puck, https://www.ledmyplace.com/products/3-5w-led-puck-light-120v-140-lumens-white; the Solid Apollo 4-puck UL E493881 kit with dimmer is $84.40, about $21 per puck, https://www.solidapollo.com/led-puck-light-kits/) or a battery puck (6-packs with remote run $15.29 to $29.99 at Walmart, so $2.50-5.00 per puck, https://www.walmart.com/ip/PEUHOP-6-Pack-LED-Puck-Lights-Remote-Battery-Operated-Cabinet-Lighting-Dimmable-Timer-Stick-Lights-Multiple-Modes-Cabinet-Lights-Closet-Kitchen-Stair/6856274192). Battery is cheapest and what the Etsy one-offs do, but it reads as a craft and customers hate changing AAs; I would ship plug-in. If he wants a bulb-style sconce with a visible Edison lamp in front of the record, a UL-listed 8 ft SPT-1 cord set with inline rotary switch and polarised plug is $7.44 to $10.85 with 12% off in bulk from Antique Lamp Supply (https://antiquelampsupply.com/products/8-ft-cord-sets-w-rotary-on-off-switches), and a keyless E26 socket adds $2-3, but that design puts 120V in a hand-built housing and I would not do it for the first batch (see the listing paragraph).

Realistic per-unit cost, excluding the record and excluding labour: at quantity 1, buying from Amazon and the box store, roughly $28-35 (power supply $14, strip $4, backplate $3, hardware $2, adhesive $2, box and padding $5). At quantity 100, buying strips and supplies in bulk and backplates cut in sheets, roughly $15-22 (power supply $8, strip $2, backplate $1.50, hardware $0.75, adhesive $1, box $3.50). Alibaba quotes for complete plug-in LED iron wall sconces with 6 ft cords at MOQ 100 land at $9-15 and $30-38 per unit depending on quality (https://electronics.alibaba.com/supplier/wall-sconce-with-switch-wholesaler, https://www.alibaba.com/showroom/wall-sconce-light.html), which is the ceiling for what a fully finished fixture should cost us, but a custom Alibaba order is a 30-60 day lead time and is not a day-one move. Add the record (section 2) and roughly 20-30 minutes of assembly per unit.

On UL/ETL: there is no federal law requiring a plug-in lamp to be listed, but it is required by code for commercial installs and some jurisdictions (Los Angeles is the one cited) treat it as illegal to sell any unlisted plug-in product (https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/ul-needed-or-not.72722/, https://mondoro.com/selling-lamps-online-listings-liability/). Using listed components does not make the finished lamp listed; a listing of the finished product means UL 153 testing, destroyed samples and an annual fee, generally reported at $10,000 or more (https://www.ul.com/services/portable-luminaires, https://www.jjrlab.com/news/cost-to-get-ul-certification-for-light-fixtures.html). The practical small-maker path is exactly what the halo design does: every electrical part is itself UL or ETL listed, the only thing we build is a wooden backplate and the record, and the mains-to-12V conversion happens inside a listed Class 2 wall adapter so no 120V is ever inside our housing. That is how most Etsy lighting makers operate, it keeps shock and fire risk in the listed part, and it should be paired with product liability insurance: ACT Insurance quotes artists and crafters from $24.25/month or $279/year for $1M per claim and $2M aggregate, priced on revenue (https://www.actinsurance.com/product-liability-insurance, https://www.actinsurance.com/blog/product-liability-insurance-cost-artists-crafters). Buy that the day the first deposit is taken, not the day the first unit ships.

On eBay a 100-LP lot sold for decorating and crafts is listed at $52 (52 cents each) and a 100+ record 1950s-80s lot at $229.99 ($2.30 each) (https://www.ebay.com/shop/bulk-vinyl-records?_nkw=bulk+vinyl+records). Dusty Groove in Chicago sells unsorted bulk boxes to set designers and decorators; the search snippet says 60 records per box at $4.99 plus shipping but the page itself blocked the scraper, so verify by phone before relying on that figure (https://www.dustygroove.com/category/bulk_vinyl_records). Record Head in West Allis, WI is a wholesaler with a $100 minimum that quotes per inquiry (https://recordhead.biz/wholesale/we-sell-wholesale-inventory/). Checkered Records sells curated playable bundles of 20 to 100 LPs for $69 to $250 (roughly $2.50-3.45 each) with an average VG+ grade and genres that exclude polka and big-band filler (https://checkeredrecords.com/product/quality-bulk-record-album-bundles-20-record-album-mystery-bulk-record-album-bundle-free-shipping/). Locally, Goodwill and Salvation Army stores in Broward price LPs at $1-3 and a Saturday of thrifting will produce 30-50 records. Important nuance: for a sconce the record is a backplate, so scratched, warped-flat-enough or scuffed copies that are worthless to collectors are perfect for us, and we should buy "play graded G/G+" or "craft grade" on purpose.

Named classic-rock titles cost real money because the label on the record, not the sleeve, is what the customer sees, and the recognisable ones are the ones they want. Discogs medians: Fleetwood Mac Rumours US 1977 pressings run $17.91 (PRC pressing) to $27.50 (Los Angeles pressing) with a VG+ asking price around $34 (https://www.discogs.com/release/526351-Fleetwood-Mac-Rumours, https://www.discogs.com/sell/release/7324434); the UK pressing has a median sold price of EUR 18.62 against EUR 35 asking, and WaxTracker notes asking prices sit 89% above what buyers actually pay (https://waxtracker.app/record/503889). Led Zeppelin IV reissues have medians of $22.64 (Greece) to $40.23 (Italy, Japan), and an original Atlantic SD 7208 in VG+ is $100-200 (https://www.discogs.com/release/1015465-Led-Zeppelin-Led-Zeppelin-IV, https://waxtracker.app/record/1015465, https://vinylai.app/guides/vinyl-record-value-guide/). Frampton Comes Alive is one of the most widely available LPs in the world and shows up in every thrift crate for $1-3 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frampton_Comes_Alive!). So the per-unit record cost range is: 50 cents to $3 for an anonymous or "we pick the era" record, $5-15 for a recognisable but common title bought in VG or lower (Frampton, Boston, Eagles Greatest Hits, Saturday Night Fever, Carole King Tapestry, any Herb Alpert), and $20-40 for a named marquee title like Rumours, Dark Side or Zeppelin in player-grade condition. The pricing in section 3 assumes two tiers so the marquee titles are an upsell, not the base.

4. Legal

The first-sale position is right in substance and needs one sharpening. Under Champion Spark Plug Co. v. Sanders (1947) and the trademark first-sale doctrine, the buyer of a genuine trademarked article can resell it, and can even recondition and resell it, so long as the public is not misled about what it is buying. The case that most resembles us is Hamilton International v. Vortic (2d Cir. 2021): Vortic built new wristwatches around restored antique Hamilton pocket-watch dials that still carried the HAMILTON mark, Hamilton sued, and Vortic won at trial and on appeal because its marketing fully disclosed that the watches were built from 100-year-old parts and nothing suggested Hamilton made or endorsed them (https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/20-3369/20-3369-2021-09-14.html, https://news.bloomberglaw.com/ip-law/watchmakers-trademark-win-spotlights-disclaimers-resale-markets). A record mounted as a sconce, described as "made from an original pressing," is that fact pattern.

Three cases cut the other way and define the line we must not cross. Chanel v. Shiver & Duke (S.D.N.Y., stipulated judgment and settlement) permanently enjoined a jeweller who turned authentic Chanel CC buttons into necklaces; the court's reasoning was that putting the button in a wholly different product was a "material difference" so first sale did not apply, and that the commercial value of the jewellery was the Chanel mark itself, which Shiver & Duke used in its own marketing (https://www.thefashionlaw.com/chanel-prevails-in-latest-round-of-trademark-lawsuit-over-upcycled-buttons/, https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/638d749b73564a2a41b4a013). Rolex v. BeckerTime (5th Cir. 2024) held that adding diamonds, aftermarket bezels and bands turned genuine Rolexes into watches "of another make" that could not be sold as Rolex, though the seller may continue customising with proper disclosures (https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/22/22-10866-CV1.pdf, https://www.thefashionlaw.com/appeals-court-delves-into-modified-watches-in-rolex-v-beckertime-trademark-case/). Nike v. MSCHF (2021) ended in a restraining order and a settlement with a full recall after MSCHF added its own content to genuine Air Max 97s and sold them as "Satan Shoes" (https://www.cnbc.com/2021/04/08/nike-settles-lawsuit-against-maker-of-lil-nas-x-satan-shoes-.html, https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/united-states/intellectual-property/trademark-infringement-nike-v-mschf-satan-shoes-case). The academic literature agrees the doctrine is unsettled for upcycled goods and that disclaimers help but do not guarantee safety (https://scholarship.law.tamu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1269&context=lawreview, https://cardozoaelj.com/2024/11/13/sustainable-fashion-meets-trademark-law-the-tension-between-upcycling-and-brand-protection-in-the-secondary-resale-market/).

The distinction that keeps us on the Vortic side rather than the Chanel side is this: we never add a mark (the label art is already on the record and we touch nothing), the product's brand is ours, and the album name appears only as a factual description of the material. Nominative fair use permits using another's mark to identify their goods when the product cannot reasonably be identified otherwise, only as much of the mark as needed is used, and nothing suggests sponsorship; a disclaimer is not legally required but helps near the line (https://www.nolo.com/legal-encyclopedia/when-you-need-permission-use-trademarks.html, https://www.starvartists.com/blog/nominative-fair-use-trademark-law). On copyright, the physical record is covered by the first-sale right in 17 U.S.C. 109, and 17 U.S.C. 113(c) specifically allows photographs of a lawfully made useful article to be used in advertisements for its sale, so product photos that show the label art are fine (https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title17-section113&num=0&edition=prelim). What is not fine is reproducing the album cover or logo as a separate graphic, printing stickers, or using the artist's name or likeness as the headline of an ad, which also raises Florida's right-of-publicity statute (Fla. Stat. 540.08) because the artist's name would then be promoting our product.

Safe wording: "Record Sconce No. 7. Handmade wall light built around an original 1977 pressing of Fleetwood Mac's Rumours. Each record is a genuine used LP; label wear is part of the piece. Not affiliated with, sponsored by or endorsed by the artist or label." Risky wording: "Fleetwood Mac Sconce," "Official Rumours Wall Light," "Limited Edition Led Zeppelin," any band name used as the product title or as the ad headline, any band logo or cover art rendered by us, any suggestion the artist signed, approved or profits. Keep our brand name large and the album name in the body copy. One hour with an IP lawyer before the marquee tier goes live is still the right call, as noted on 11/9.

On pre-orders, the FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule (16 CFR Part 435) applies to anything ordered online regardless of payment method; we must have a reasonable basis for any shipping date we advertise, 30 days is the default if we state none, the clock starts when we receive a properly completed order including a partial payment, and if we miss the date we must send a delay notice offering a revised date or a refund, with silence counting as consent only for the first delay of 30 days or less; refunds for non-credit payments are due within seven working days and for card payments within one billing cycle (https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guide-ftcs-mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule). The FTC's business guide says the Rule does not bite on a pre-order only if we clearly disclose that the merchandise is planned and may not ship, and notify buyers within a reasonable time if the plan changes. Because of this, the deposit page should say in plain words: "Deposits are fully refundable at any time. First batch ships the week of [date]; if we cannot ship by then you will be told and may cancel for a full refund." Pick a date we can hit with a two-week buffer; "ships in 6-8 weeks" from a day-one deposit is a reasonable basis if parts are a 10-day order and assembly is 100 units at 25 minutes each.

On scarcity: the FTC's "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" report (September 2022) names "False Low Stock Message," "Baseless Countdown Timer" and "False Limited Time Message" as deceptive patterns and cites claims that an item is almost sold out when there is ample supply as actionable under Section 5 (https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light); the FTC's dark-patterns enforcement has produced over $2.9 billion in penalties and refunds since 2022, including the $2.5 billion Amazon settlement finalised September 2025 (https://privacyterms.io/dark-patterns-statistics-2026, https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/dark-patterns-lead-to-enforcement-spotlight-key-compliance-steps-for-businesses/). Meta's Advertising Standards prohibit "deceptive or misleading practices," review the landing page as well as the ad, and require the products and claims in the ad to match the landing page (https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/). Meta's catalog spec recognises "preorder" and "available for order" as legitimate availability states (https://keepersdigital.com/en-us/resources/paid-social/meta-catalog-troubleshooting/). So "sold out" is only lawful if a real, finite drop actually sold out. The 11/9 plan to make it true, a tiny real first drop (10-20 deposits to friends and a soft post) that genuinely sells out before the ad reads "First drop sold out. Join the waitlist for batch two," is the correct and defensible way to give Austin his headline. The page must then show the real sold-out drop (count and date) and must never show a countdown or a stock counter that is not wired to real numbers. "First batch of 100" must be a real cap: deposit number 101 goes on a batch-two list.

6. Risks

First, demand at the price does not exist outside Austin's head, and the $20 resin fakes anchor the category low. Mitigation: the deposit test is the mitigation; set a kill line of $80 per deposit after $150 spent, and lead every creative with the real vinyl and the halo so no one confuses it with the resin toy.

Second, a label or artist's management sends a cease-and-desist over the marquee tier. Mitigation: brand-first naming with the album in body copy only, no logos or cover art, the Vortic-style disclosure on every product page, the one-hour IP review before the marquee menu goes live, and a two-day rule to pull any specific title on request rather than fight.

Third, a fire or shock incident with a hand-built fixture. Mitigation: Class 2 low voltage only behind the record, every electrical component UL or ETL listed, no 120V inside our housing, the ACT product liability policy bought before the first ship, and a one-page safety card in the box.
